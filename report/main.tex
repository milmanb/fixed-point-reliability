\begin{abstract}
A learned restoration model does not signal when its output is wrong. Idempotent networks
are trained so that $f(f(y)) = f(y)$, and recent work reads a small idempotence residual as a
sign of a reliable prediction. We test this reading in a controlled denoising setting on
Fashion-MNIST. We compare three reference-free signals, the idempotence residual, the
displacement and the measurement residual, with the true per-image error under noise, blur
and masking. For exact projectors the residual is zero by construction. For learned denoisers
it ranks errors within a known corruption (Spearman $\rho \approx 0.7$) and ranks them better
than the other signals when corruptions are mixed. It still misses the largest failures: under
random pixel masks the model outputs dim, plausible images that are near fixed points, with
top-decile error and typical residuals. Training for idempotence with our loss collapses the
model to a constant image with zero residual. \todo{warm-up result}. Simple brightness and
severity baselines show how evaluations of such signals can be inflated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Restoration networks are often applied where the clean image is unknown. When such a network
fails, its output can still look plausible, so the failure cannot be seen in the image alone.
A signal that flags unreliable outputs without ground truth would therefore be useful.

Idempotent Generative Networks (IGN)~\cite{shocher2024ign} train a network $f$ to map inputs
onto a target manifold, so that $f(f(y)) = f(y)$. Related methods use the deviation from this
property as a confidence score. ZigZag~\cite{durasov2024zigzag} and
IT$^3$~\cite{durasov2025it3} report that a self-consistency residual correlates strongly with
prediction error, and NAIGN~\cite{aljaff2025naign} proposes the drift for out-of-distribution
detection. It is tempting to conclude that an output which is a fixed point is also a correct
output.

This conclusion does not hold in general. An exact projector satisfies $P(P(y)) = P(y)$ for
every input, including inputs that it maps to a wrong image. The open question is how the
residual behaves for learned models, which are only approximately idempotent, and whether
training for idempotence changes this behaviour.

We study the question in a small, controlled setting with known corruptions, where the true
error is available offline. Our contributions are:
(i)~an evaluation protocol for reference-free signals that separates ranking within a
corruption level from ranking across levels, with baselines for image brightness and severity;
(ii)~evidence that the idempotence residual of a learned denoiser is informative for familiar
corruptions but blind to its main failure under an unseen operator;
(iii)~an ablation of explicit idempotence training, which collapses the model to a constant
output unless the loss weight is ramped up \todo{and what the ramped models show}.

\paragraph{Related work.}
Idempotence has been used to train one-step generative
models~\cite{shocher2024ign,aljaff2025naign} and to distill diffusion
models~\cite{zaman2025sign}. In supervised prediction, self-consistency residuals serve as
uncertainty estimates~\cite{durasov2024zigzag,durasov2025it3}. The Pearson correlation of
$-0.94$ that IT$^3$ reports on ImageNet-C is computed per inference batch, not per image;
aggregate correlations of this kind can hide weak per-image ranking. Closest to our setting,
Huang et al.~\cite{huang2023cycle} estimate the uncertainty of deblurring and super-resolution
networks from forward-backward cycle residuals, which include $\norm{A f(y) - y}$. Classical
reference-free error estimates include Stein's unbiased risk estimate
(SURE)~\cite{stein1981sure} and its Monte-Carlo version for black-box
denoisers~\cite{ramani2008mcsure}. Self-supervised losses estimate the error under a
$J$-invariance assumption~\cite{batson2019noise2self}, and for a minimum mean squared error
denoiser the posterior covariance equals the Jacobian of the denoiser times the noise
variance~\cite{manor2024posterior}, which is why we also report the divergence.
Data-consistency checks cannot detect errors in the null
space of the forward operator~\cite{bhadra2021hallucinations}, and restorations with perfect
perceptual quality pay a price in distortion~\cite{blau2018perception}. Our scope is narrower:
we measure what the fixed-point residual tells about the error of each image, for exact and
learned projectors.

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Setting.}
Let $x$ be a clean image and $y = A_s x + n$ an observation, with a known linear operator $A_s$
and Gaussian noise $n$. A model $f$ returns an estimate $f(y)$, and $x$ is not available at test
time. We use four corruption families with 13 levels: additive Gaussian noise ($A_s = I$,
$\sigma \in \{0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5\}$), Gaussian blur (kernel standard deviation 0.5, 1, 1.5 or 2
pixels), random pixel masks (25\%, 50\% or 75\% of the pixels set to zero) and one random square
mask with a side of 8 or 14 pixels. Blur and masks add weak noise ($\sigma = 0.02$).

\paragraph{Signals.}
With $|\Omega|$ the number of pixels, we compare
\begin{align*}
  g(y) &= \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{f(f(y)) - f(y)}_1, & d(y) &= \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{f(y) - y}_1,\\
  r_A(y) &= \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{A_s f(y) - y}_1, & e(y) &= \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{f(y) - x}_1.
\end{align*}
The idempotence residual $g$ and the displacement $d$ need only $f$ and $y$. The measurement
residual $r_A$ also needs $A_s$, and equals $d$ for denoising. The true error $e$ is used only
for evaluation. For Gaussian noise we also compute
$\mathrm{SURE}(y) = \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{f(y)-y}_2^2 - \sigma^2
+ \tfrac{2\sigma^2}{|\Omega|}\operatorname{div} f(y)$, an unbiased estimate of the mean squared
error of each image~\cite{stein1981sure}. We estimate the divergence with four Rademacher probes
and forward-mode differentiation, following the Monte-Carlo approach of~\cite{ramani2008mcsure}.

\paragraph{Models.}
The denoiser $f$ is a convolutional autoencoder with 602k parameters: three convolutions down
to $7\times7$, a 64-dimensional bottleneck, a mirrored decoder and a sigmoid output. It has no
normalization layers, so applying $f$ to its own output is not affected by batch statistics.
We train on 55{,}000 Fashion-MNIST~\cite{xiao2017fashion} training images with Gaussian noise,
$\sigma \sim \mathcal{U}[0.05, 0.25]$, for 15 epochs with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, cosine
decay, batch size 256). The loss, averaged over pixels, is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \norm{f(y) - x}_1 + \lid\,\norm{f(f(y)) - f(y)}_1,
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
with gradients through both applications of $f$. We train $\lid \in \{0, 1\}$ with three seeds
each and keep the other 5{,}000 training images for validation. As exact references we use two
projectors fitted to the training set: onto a sphere around the training mean (radial), and onto
the top-$k$ principal subspace (PCA-$k$).

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
We use the 10{,}000 test images. For each signal we report Spearman's $\rho$ with $e$ within each
corruption level, and pooled over all 13 levels, which corresponds to an unknown corruption. We
also report the AUROC for detecting the worst quartile of $e$. Two confounds need care. Under
masking, the error grows with the amount of content in an image, so we add the brightness
$b(y) = \operatorname{mean}(y)$ as a model-free baseline and report partial correlations
given~$b$. In pooled scores, a signal can rank images mostly by severity, so we compare it with
the same signal replaced by its median over each level. Confidence intervals (95\%) come from
1000 bootstrap resamples of the test images; in pooled scores all levels of an image are
resampled together. With 10{,}000 images, the intervals on $\rho$ are about $\pm 0.01$.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Exact projectors have zero residual}
For the radial and PCA projectors, $g < 2\cdot10^{-15}$ on every image and level, while the mean
error lies between 0.04 and 0.23. A constant signal cannot rank images. The displacement behaves
differently: it ranks errors well when the fixed-point set is tight (PCA-16, median within-level
$\rho = 0.91$) and can reverse when it is not (radial projector at $\sigma = 0.2$,
$\rho(d, e) = -0.46$).

garment that it treats as a fixed point (Fig.~\ref{fig:failures}). Among images with top-10\%
error and below-median $g$, 97--98\% come from pixel masks, in all three models. Box masks behave
differently: their worst outputs have large residuals.

\subsection{Training for idempotence}
With $\lid = 1$ from the first step, all three seeds collapse within one epoch to a constant
output. The validation error is 0.2107, the error of the per-pixel median training image
(0.2106), and $g$ is zero. The collapsed model is exactly idempotent and useless as a denoiser.
The loss at this point is about 0.21, far above its value at the $\lid = 0$ solution (about
0.06), so the collapse is a failure of optimization rather than the minimum of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}. A likely cause is that a network at initialization is nearly constant, and
any input dependence first raises the residual. When $\lid$ is ramped linearly from 0 to 1 over
five epochs, training does not collapse. \todo{error, residual and ranking of the ramped models}.

\section{Discussion}
A small idempotence residual is weak evidence of a correct restoration. For exact projectors it
carries no information. For learned denoisers it ranks errors usefully within familiar
corruptions, and it is more robust to the noise level than the displacement. Its low values,
however, include the largest failures under an unseen operator. There the model maps the input
to an image that it reproduces, and the residual cannot tell this image from the correct one.
Explicit idempotence training shows the same effect in the extreme: a constant map has zero
residual. \todo{update with the ramped models}.

The baselines matter as much as the signals. Brightness explains most of the within-level
ranking under masks, and pooling over levels inflates correlations, as in batch-level analyses.
When evaluating a reference-free reliability signal, we recommend reporting per-level scores
together with severity and content baselines.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
We use one small dataset, one architecture and synthetic corruptions. The models are trained on
noise only, so blur and masks test unseen operators. We tested one gradient routing for the
idempotence term and one warm-up schedule; stop-gradient variants as in IGN may behave
differently.
