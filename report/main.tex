\begin{abstract}
Idempotent networks are trained so that $f(f(y)) = f(y)$, and recent work reads a small
idempotence residual as a sign of a reliable prediction. We test this reading on small Fashion-MNIST denoisers against the true
per-image error. The residual is zero for any exact projector, whatever
the error, and a first-order expansion shows that it probes the Jacobian along the model's own
displacement. For learned models it ranks errors within a corruption level (Spearman
$\rho = 0.70$ for a bottleneck autoencoder, 0.86 with skip connections). Yet each architecture has
an unseen corruption under which its outputs are stable but wrong and the residual misses them:
pixel masks for the bottleneck model, large box masks for the skip model. A strong idempotence
penalty makes the residual a worse signal in both architectures ($\rho$ 0.62 and 0.63), and
without a warm-up it collapses both to constant images. For the bottleneck model, calibrating the
residual per level lifts its pooled ranking from 0.55 to 0.89, but predicting each level's mean
error already reaches 0.77.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Restoration networks are often applied where the clean image is unknown. When they fail, the
output can still look plausible, so a signal that flags unreliable outputs without ground truth
would be useful.

Idempotent Generative Networks (IGN)~\cite{shocher2024ign} train a network $f$ so that real data
are fixed points, $f(x) = x$, and other inputs land on that set, $f(f(y)) = f(y)$. Related
methods read the deviation from this property as a confidence
score~\cite{durasov2024zigzag,durasov2025it3,aljaff2025naign}. It is tempting to conclude that an
output which is a fixed point is also a correct one. This does not hold in general: an exact
projector satisfies $P(P(y)) = P(y)$ for every input, including inputs that it maps to a wrong
image. We ask how the residual behaves for learned models, which are only approximately
idempotent, and how training for idempotence changes it, in a controlled setting with known
errors. We find that (i)~the residual is a useful signal within a corruption
level, but each of two architectures has a corruption under which it misses the worst errors;
(ii)~a strong idempotence penalty makes the residual a worse signal, and without a warm-up it
collapses the model to a constant image; (iii)~brightness and level baselines and per-level calibration are
needed to read such signals correctly.

\paragraph{Related work.}
Idempotence has been used to train one-step generative
models~\cite{shocher2024ign,aljaff2025naign,zaman2025sign}, and NAIGN~\cite{aljaff2025naign}
reads the drift of a network as a distance to the data manifold. In supervised prediction, ZigZag~\cite{durasov2024zigzag} reports
per-sample correlations above 0.9 between a self-consistency residual and the loss, while the
$-0.94$ that IT$^3$~\cite{durasov2025it3} reports between idempotence error and accuracy on
ImageNet-C is computed per inference batch. Closest to our setting, Huang et
al.~\cite{huang2023cycle} predict the uncertainty of deblurring and super-resolution networks from
forward-backward cycles, whose features include $\norm{A_s f(y) - y}$. Unlike them, we test the
idempotence residual itself and how idempotence training changes it. For denoising, SURE and its
Monte-Carlo form estimate the error without references~\cite{stein1981sure,ramani2008mcsure}.

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Setting.}
Let $x$ be a clean image and $y = A_s x + n$ an observation, with a known linear operator $A_s$ for
corruption $s$ and Gaussian noise $n$; a model $f$ must estimate $x$ without seeing it. A \emph{level} is one operator at one strength, and a \emph{family} groups the levels
of one operator. We use 13 levels in four families: Gaussian noise ($A_s = I$,
$\sigma \in \{0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5\}$), Gaussian blur (width 0.5, 1, 1.5 or 2 pixels), pixel masks
(each pixel zeroed with probability 0.25, 0.5 or 0.75) and box masks (one random square of side 8
or 14). Blur and masks add weak noise ($\sigma = 0.02$), which the masks apply before zeroing.
Models are trained on Gaussian noise with $\sigma \le 0.25$ only, so $\sigma \ge 0.3$, blur and
masks are unseen.

\paragraph{Signals.}
With $|\Omega|$ the number of pixels, we compare
\begin{align*}
  g(y) &= \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{f(f(y)) - f(y)}_1, & d(y) &= \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{f(y) - y}_1,\\
  r_A(y) &= \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{A_s f(y) - y}_1, & e(y) &= \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{f(y) - x}_1.
\end{align*}
The residual $g$ and the displacement $d$ need only $f$ and $y$; the measurement residual $r_A$
also needs $A_s$ and equals $d$ for denoising. For
Gaussian noise we also compute Stein's unbiased estimate of the squared error~\cite{stein1981sure},
$\mathrm{SURE}(y) = \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{f(y)-y}_2^2 - \sigma^2
+ \tfrac{2\sigma^2}{|\Omega|}\operatorname{div} f(y)$, with the divergence from four Rademacher
probes and finite differences ($\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$), a variant of~\cite{ramani2008mcsure}.

\paragraph{Why the residual can be blind.}
Write $\delta(y) = f(y) - y$ for the displacement. A first-order expansion of the second
application of $f$ gives
\begin{equation}
  g(y) = \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{f(y + \delta) - f(y)}_1
       \approx \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{J_f(y)\,\delta(y)}_1,
  \label{eq:taylor}
\end{equation}
so the residual probes the Jacobian along the one direction in which the model has just moved.
For an exact projector onto a smooth set, $\delta$ is normal to that set while $J_f$ projects onto
its tangent space, so $J_f\delta = 0$ whatever the error. A learned model can only be informative
where $J_f$ does not cancel its own displacement.

\paragraph{Models.}
The \emph{bottleneck} model is a convolutional autoencoder with 602k parameters: three
convolutions (base width 32, SiLU, stride 2 after the first) down to $7\times7$, a 64-dimensional
bottleneck, a mirrored decoder and a sigmoid output. The \emph{skip} model has the same
convolutions with skip connections and no bottleneck (230k parameters), so it can copy detail from
its input. We train on 55{,}000 Fashion-MNIST~\cite{xiao2017fashion}
images with $\sigma \sim \mathcal{U}[0.05, 0.25]$, for 15 epochs with Adam (learning rate
$10^{-3}$, cosine decay, batch 256), and minimize
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \norm{f(y) - x}_1 + \lid\,\norm{f(f(y)) - f(y)}_1,
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
averaged over pixels. Both models are trained with $\lid \in \{0, 1\}$ and the bottleneck model
also with $\lid = 0.1$; three seeds each. For $\lid > 0$ we ramp $\lid$ linearly from 0 over five
epochs (a warm-up); one run per model omits it. Gradients normally flow through both applications
of $f$. \emph{Inner-only} training freezes the weights of the outer $f$ in $f(f(y))$, as in IGN;
\emph{outer-only} training freezes the inner output. As exact references we project onto a sphere
around the training mean (radial) and onto the top 16, 64 or 256 principal components (PCA).

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
On 10{,}000 test images we report Spearman's $\rho$ between a signal and $e$ within each level and
pooled over all levels, which corresponds to an unknown corruption, and the AUROC for detecting
the worst quartile of $e$. An image is \emph{flagged} when its signal is in the top quartile over
all levels, so a random score flags 25\% of any subset. Under masking the error grows with the
content of an image, so we add the brightness $b(y) = \operatorname{mean}(y)$ as a model-free
baseline and report partial correlations given $b$. For calibration we fit isotonic regression of
$e$ on a signal, per level or per family, on half the test images, score the other half, and
compare with a predictor that returns each level's mean error. Bootstrap 95\% intervals have a
half-width of about 0.01.

\subsection{Exact projectors}
For the radial and PCA projectors $g < 2\cdot10^{-15}$, and where we computed the right side of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:taylor} (radial and PCA-64 under noise) it is at most $10^{-12}$, the round-off of the
finite differences, while the mean error
lies between 0.04 and 0.23. The residual cannot rank these errors at all.

\subsection{Learned models and their blind spots}
For the bottleneck model, $g$ ranks errors within a level with $\rho = 0.70$
(Table~\ref{tab:main}), and Eq.~\eqref{eq:taylor} holds in rank (Spearman 0.81 to 0.97 between
$g$ and its linearization for $\sigma \le 0.3$). Under noise the residual holds up as the noise grows
(0.75 at $\sigma = 0.1$, 0.72 at 0.5), while $d$ falls from 0.93 to 0.28 and SURE from 0.91 to 0.40
(against squared error, SURE ranks best for $\sigma \le 0.3$). The
skip model's residual ranks errors better at 11 of the 13 levels, and its partial $\rho$ is higher
at the same 11 levels for every seed; its median is still lower because of the box masks. Its displacement is worse (0.49), and negative for
$\sigma \ge 0.2$.
Brightness alone reaches a median within-level $\rho$ of 0.51 to 0.55, and 0.96 under pixel
masks, so raw scores under masks and blur overstate the signals.

Pooled over all levels, $g$ reaches $\rho = 0.55$ and an AUROC of 0.69 for the bottleneck model,
against $\rho$ of 0.12 for $d$ and 0.02 for $r_A$, whose scales differ between families; replacing
$g$ by its median per level still gives 0.46. The pooled score hides a blind spot
(Fig.~\ref{fig:failures}). Under pixel masks with 75\% dropped the bottleneck model's mean error is
0.234, five times that at $\sigma = 0.1$, while its mean residual barely moves (0.014 against 0.011):
it turns sparse dots into a dim garment and treats it as a fixed point. Pixel masks hold 60\% of
the worst-quartile errors, and $g$ flags 18\% of them, fewer than a random score. The skip model
has the opposite blind spot. Under box masks its within-level $\rho$ drops to 0.29 and 0.12
(bottleneck: 0.83 and 0.71), it flags under 1\% of the worst box-mask errors, and 83--87\% of its
images with top-10\% error and below-median $g$ are large boxes. It also flags only 14--16\% of
its worst blur errors, although those outputs are not stable.

\subsection{Training for idempotence}
\label{sec:idem}
Without a warm-up, $\lid = 1$ collapses both architectures within one epoch to a constant image.
The bottleneck model settles on the per-pixel median training image (validation error 0.2106 for
both, residual $10^{-6}$) and the skip model on a black image (validation error 0.2885, the mean
pixel value; residual $10^{-10}$). Their losses, 0.21 and 0.29, are far above the values at the
$\lid = 0$ solutions (about 0.06 and 0.05), so these are failures of optimization. At
initialization the bottleneck model's output varies by $5\cdot10^{-6}$ across inputs and the skip
model's by $8\cdot10^{-3}$ (clean images: 0.27), so a start as constant as the bottleneck's is not
required.

With the warm-up, mean error changes little (0.092 to 0.095; 0.086 to 0.084). The residual falls by 40\% and 61\% and becomes a
worse signal in both architectures: within-level $\rho$ falls from 0.70 to 0.62 and from 0.86 to
0.63, with no overlap between seeds, and so do the noise, partial and pooled scores and
the pixel-mask flagged share. Only its box-mask flagged share rises. A weight of
0.1 leaves the within-level $\rho$ unchanged (0.71 against 0.70) and changes the other
Table~\ref{tab:main} scores by at most 0.03, except the pixel-mask flagged share (18\% to 13\%). All
three routings lower the within-level $\rho$ (0.62, 0.61 inner-only, 0.65
outer-only). Outer-only training also reproduces the losses in partial $\rho$ (0.54), pooled
$\rho$ (0.50) and the pixel-mask flagged share (11\%), while inner-only training keeps them
(0.61, 0.53, 22\%). These three losses appear only when gradients reach the outer application,
which shrinks $g$ more (by 26\% against 6\%).

\subsection{Calibration}
Each signal has a different scale in each family. After per-level
calibration, pooled $\rho$ is 0.89 for $g$, 0.93 for $d$ and 0.94 for $r_A$ in the bottleneck
model, and 0.92--0.93 in the skip model. The level-mean predictor already reaches 0.77 and 0.84, so
the per-image signals add 0.08 to 0.17. When only the family is known, $g$ is the best calibrated
signal in both models (0.84 and 0.79).

\section{Discussion}
In our setting, a small idempotence residual is weak evidence of a correct restoration. For a
projector it carries no information. For the two learned models it is a useful signal within a
level, but each produces stable but wrong outputs under at least one unseen corruption, and the
residual misses most of them. Which corruption this is depends on the architecture, so a residual
validated on some corruptions need not transfer to others.

With $\lid = 1$, the residual became a worse signal in both architectures while the models still
restored well. A diagnostic that is also optimized as a loss can lose information; here the losses in
brightness-controlled and pooled ranking needed gradients through the outer application. Methods that
minimize the residual at test time~\cite{durasov2025it3} may face the same issue; we did not test
this. No signal compares corruptions without calibration, and a level-mean predictor already
reaches most of the calibrated performance.

\paragraph{Limitations and deviations from the proposal.}
We proposed to calibrate the signals; we evaluate the ranking of calibrated values, without a
coverage guarantee, and report the worst-quartile AUROC pooled only. We added box masks, SURE, the
baselines, the second architecture and the ablations, and $\lid > 0$ needed a warm-up. The evidence comes from one small dataset, two small models of different size, three seeds
per setting and one full collapse run per architecture (two shorter bottleneck runs also
collapsed). The black background of Fashion-MNIST makes
brightness a strong baseline.
