\begin{abstract}
A learned restoration model does not signal when its output is wrong. Idempotent networks
are trained so that $f(f(y)) = f(y)$, and recent work reads a small idempotence residual as a
sign of a reliable prediction. We test this reading in a controlled denoising setting on
Fashion-MNIST. We compare three reference-free signals, the idempotence residual, the
displacement and the measurement residual, with the true per-image error under noise, blur
and masking. For exact projectors the residual is zero by construction. For learned denoisers
it ranks errors within a known corruption (Spearman $\rho \approx 0.7$) and ranks them better
than the other signals when corruptions are mixed. It still misses the largest failures: under
random pixel masks the model outputs dim, plausible images that are near fixed points, with
top-decile error and typical residuals. Training for idempotence with our loss collapses the
model to a constant image with zero residual. With the weight ramped up over the first epochs
the model trains normally, halves its residual, and makes that residual a worse signal: the
correlation within a level drops from 0.70 to 0.62, and the share of the worst masking errors
it flags drops from 18\% to 10\%. Simple brightness and severity baselines show how
evaluations of such signals can be inflated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Restoration networks are often applied where the clean image is unknown. When such a network
fails, its output can still look plausible, so the failure cannot be seen in the image alone.
A signal that flags unreliable outputs without ground truth would therefore be useful.

Idempotent Generative Networks (IGN)~\cite{shocher2024ign} train a network $f$ to map inputs
onto a target manifold, so that $f(f(y)) = f(y)$. Related methods use the deviation from this
property as a confidence score. ZigZag~\cite{durasov2024zigzag} and
IT$^3$~\cite{durasov2025it3} report that a self-consistency residual correlates strongly with
prediction error, and NAIGN~\cite{aljaff2025naign} proposes the drift for out-of-distribution
detection. It is tempting to conclude that an output which is a fixed point is also a correct
output.

This conclusion does not hold in general. An exact projector satisfies $P(P(y)) = P(y)$ for
every input, including inputs that it maps to a wrong image. The open question is how the
residual behaves for learned models, which are only approximately idempotent, and whether
training for idempotence changes this behaviour.

We study the question in a small, controlled setting with known corruptions, where the true
error is available offline. Our contributions are:
(i)~an evaluation protocol for reference-free signals that separates ranking within a
corruption level from ranking across levels, with baselines for image brightness and severity;
(ii)~evidence that the idempotence residual of a learned denoiser is informative for familiar
corruptions but blind to its main failure under an unseen operator;
(iii)~an ablation of explicit idempotence training, which collapses the model to a constant
output unless the loss weight is ramped up, and which makes the residual both smaller and
less informative when it is.

\paragraph{Related work.}
Idempotence has been used to train one-step generative
models~\cite{shocher2024ign,aljaff2025naign} and to distill diffusion
models~\cite{zaman2025sign}. In supervised prediction, self-consistency residuals serve as
uncertainty estimates~\cite{durasov2024zigzag,durasov2025it3}; the correlation of $-0.94$ that
IT$^3$ reports on ImageNet-C is computed per inference batch, not per image. Closest to our
setting, Huang et al.~\cite{huang2023cycle} estimate the uncertainty of deblurring and
super-resolution networks from forward-backward cycle residuals, which include
$\norm{A f(y) - y}$. Reference-free error estimates for denoising include
SURE~\cite{stein1981sure} and its Monte-Carlo form for black-box
models~\cite{ramani2008mcsure}; for a minimum mean squared error denoiser the posterior
covariance is the Jacobian times the noise variance~\cite{manor2024posterior}, which is why we
also report the divergence. Data
consistency cannot detect errors in the null space of the
operator~\cite{bhadra2021hallucinations}, and restorations with perfect perceptual quality pay
in distortion~\cite{blau2018perception}. We measure what the fixed-point residual adds per
image, for exact and learned projectors.

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Setting.}
Let $x$ be a clean image and $y = A_s x + n$ an observation, with a known linear operator $A_s$
and Gaussian noise $n$. A model $f$ returns an estimate $f(y)$, and $x$ is not available at test
time. We use 13 corruption levels: Gaussian noise ($A_s = I$, $\sigma \in \{0.1, 0.2, 0.3,
0.5\}$), Gaussian blur (width 0.5 to 2 pixels), random pixel masks (25, 50 or 75\% of pixels
zeroed) and a random square mask (side 8 or 14). Blur and masks add weak noise
($\sigma = 0.02$).

\paragraph{Signals.}
With $|\Omega|$ the number of pixels, we compare
\begin{align*}
  g(y) &= \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{f(f(y)) - f(y)}_1, & d(y) &= \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{f(y) - y}_1,\\
  r_A(y) &= \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{A_s f(y) - y}_1, & e(y) &= \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{f(y) - x}_1.
\end{align*}
The idempotence residual $g$ and the displacement $d$ need only $f$ and $y$. The measurement
residual $r_A$ also needs $A_s$, and equals $d$ for denoising. The true error $e$ is used only
for evaluation. For Gaussian noise we also compute
$\mathrm{SURE}(y) = \tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\norm{f(y)-y}_2^2 - \sigma^2
+ \tfrac{2\sigma^2}{|\Omega|}\operatorname{div} f(y)$, an unbiased estimate of the mean squared
error of each image~\cite{stein1981sure}. We estimate the divergence with the Monte-Carlo
estimator of~\cite{ramani2008mcsure}: four Rademacher probes $v$ and the finite difference
$v^\top (f(y + \varepsilon v) - f(y)) / \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$. On our models
this agrees with exact forward-mode products to a relative $10^{-4}$.

\paragraph{Models.}
The denoiser $f$ is a convolutional autoencoder with 602k parameters: three convolutions down
to $7\times7$, a 64-dimensional bottleneck, a mirrored decoder and a sigmoid output. It has no
normalization layers, so applying $f$ to its own output is not affected by batch statistics.
We train on 55{,}000 Fashion-MNIST~\cite{xiao2017fashion} training images with Gaussian noise,
$\sigma \sim \mathcal{U}[0.05, 0.25]$, for 15 epochs with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, cosine
decay, batch size 256). The loss, averaged over pixels, is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \norm{f(y) - x}_1 + \lid\,\norm{f(f(y)) - f(y)}_1,
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
with gradients through both applications of $f$. We train $\lid \in \{0, 1\}$ with three seeds
each and keep the other 5{,}000 training images for validation. For $\lid = 1$ the weight is
ramped linearly from 0 over the first five epochs; Section~\ref{sec:idem} shows what happens
without the ramp. As exact references we use two
projectors fitted to the training set: onto a sphere around the training mean (radial), and onto
the top-$k$ principal subspace (PCA-$k$).

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
On the 10{,}000 test images we report Spearman's $\rho$ between each signal and $e$, within each
corruption level and pooled over all 13 levels, which corresponds to an unknown corruption, plus
the AUROC for detecting the worst quartile of $e$. Two confounds need care. Under masking the
error grows with the amount of content in an image, so we add the brightness
$b(y) = \operatorname{mean}(y)$ as a model-free baseline and report partial correlations
given~$b$. In pooled scores a signal can rank images mostly by severity, so we compare it with
the same signal replaced by its median over each level. Confidence intervals (95\%) come from
1000 bootstrap resamples of test images, resampling all levels of an image together; they are
about $\pm 0.01$ wide.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Exact projectors have zero residual}
For the radial and PCA projectors $g < 2\cdot10^{-15}$ everywhere, while the mean error lies
between 0.04 and 0.23, so the residual cannot rank images at all. The displacement ranks errors
well when the fixed-point set is tight (PCA-16, median $\rho = 0.91$) and can reverse when it is
not (radial at $\sigma = 0.2$, $\rho = -0.46$).

Table~\ref{tab:rank} summarizes the models. For $\lid = 0$, $g$ ranks errors with $\rho$ between
0.72 and 0.75 inside every noise level and does not degrade as the noise grows, while the
displacement falls from 0.93 at $\sigma = 0.1$ to 0.28 at the held-out $\sigma = 0.5$ and SURE
falls from 0.91 to 0.40. Pooled over all 13 levels, $g$ reaches $\rho = 0.55$, against 0.12 for
$d$ and 0.02 for $r_A$, whose scales differ between noise, blur and masks.

The pooled score hides a systematic failure (Fig.~\ref{fig:scatter}). Under pixel masks with 75\%
of the pixels dropped, the mean error is 0.234, five times the error at $\sigma = 0.1$ (0.043).
The mean residual barely changes (0.013 against 0.011). The model turns sparse dots into a dim
garment that it treats as a fixed point (Fig.~\ref{fig:failures}). Pixel masks hold 60\% of the
worst-quartile errors and $g$ flags 18\% of them, against 74\% for box masks and 82\% under
noise. Among the images with top-10\% error and below-median $g$, 98\% are pixel masks.

Raw within-level scores overstate every signal. The brightness $b$ alone reaches a median
within-level $\rho$ of 0.51, and 0.96 under pixel masks, where the error grows with the amount
of content. The partial columns of Table~\ref{tab:rank} remove this effect: $r_A$ keeps most of
its ranking (0.79 to 0.76), while $d$ loses more (0.76 to 0.57).

\subsection{Training for idempotence}
\label{sec:idem}
With $\lid = 1$ from the first step, all three seeds collapse within one epoch to a constant
output. The validation error is 0.2107, the error of the per-pixel median training image
(0.2106), and the residual falls to $1.4\cdot10^{-6}$. The collapse held for all 15 epochs. This
model is exactly idempotent and useless as a denoiser. Its loss is about 0.21, far above the
value at the $\lid = 0$ solution (about 0.06), so the collapse is a failure of optimization
rather than the minimum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}. A likely cause is that a network at
initialization is nearly constant, so any input dependence first raises the residual.

Ramping $\lid$ from 0 to 1 over five epochs avoids the collapse. Those models restore almost as
well as $\lid = 0$ (mean error 0.095 against 0.092 over all conditions) and halve the residual
(0.0088 against 0.0146). The residual also becomes a worse signal. Its correlation with the error
drops from 0.70 to 0.62 within a level, from 0.75 to 0.60 within noise levels, and the share of
the worst pixel-mask errors it flags drops from 18\% to 10\%. The three seeds of the two groups
do not overlap on any of these numbers. The count of images with top-10\% error and
below-median residual rises from 3748 to 4446 out of 130000. The same training improves the
other signals (Table~\ref{tab:rank}), which matches the projector experiment: a tighter
fixed-point set makes the displacement more informative.

\section{Discussion}
A small idempotence residual is weak evidence of a correct restoration. For exact projectors it
carries no information. For learned denoisers it ranks errors usefully within familiar
corruptions, and it is the only signal here that survives pooling over corruption types. Its low
values, however, include the largest failures under an unseen operator. There the model maps the
input to an image that it reproduces, and the residual cannot tell that image from the correct
one.

Training for idempotence does not repair this, it deepens it. The residual halves, its ranking
quality drops, and the model produces more stable but wrong outputs. A quantity that is used as
a diagnostic and optimized as a loss stops being a diagnostic. Without a warm-up the same
objective produces a constant map, which is the end point of that process.

When the operator is known, the measurement residual is the better signal, and it keeps most of
its ranking after brightness is controlled for. Under Gaussian noise of known strength, SURE
estimates the error directly and ranks best at the training noise levels. The baselines matter
as much as the signals: brightness explains most of the within-level ranking under masks, and
pooling over levels inflates correlations, as in batch-level analyses. We recommend reporting
per-level scores together with severity and content baselines.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
We use one small dataset, one architecture and synthetic corruptions, and train on noise only,
so blur and masks test unseen operators. We tested one gradient routing and one warm-up
schedule; IGN-style stop-gradients may behave differently.
